# 5. Teaching Computer Science - Niklaus Wirth

**Speaker**: Niklaus Wirth  
**URL**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BJIqHIYSDrk
**Duration**: 15 minutes, 10 seconds  
**Channel**: IEEEComputerSociety

---

Niklaus Wirth discusses his journey in computer science, focusing on his motivation to create programming languages suitable for teaching due to the inadequacy of existing tools like Algol and Fortran (1:01-1:43). This led to the development of Pascal (1:56), which gained significant traction with the advent of microcomputers and affordable compilers (3:21-4:24). He then explains the evolution to Modula-2, designed for system development with crucial features like module interfaces and separate compilation (7:07-8:04), and subsequently to Oberon, which emphasized simplicity and object-oriented features (8:23-9:26). Wirth also details his venture into hardware design, driven by the desire for personal computers after his Xerox sabbatical, leading to the creation of the Lilith and Ceres computers, which applied software design principles to hardware (10:25-12:00). He concludes by explaining his recent work with FPGAs to build a simplified RISC processor and an Oberon compiler for teaching purposes (12:25-14:50).

Here's a breakdown of the key information:

Early Programming Languages: Wirth found Algol to have a "lousy implementation" (1:19-1:23) and Fortran "unsuitable for teaching" (1:35-1:39), prompting him to create a new language.
Development of Pascal: His decision to create Pascal (1:56-2:01) stemmed from the need for a better teaching tool, and it was first used in an introductory programming course in 1971 (2:36-2:43).
Pascal's Breakthrough: The widespread adoption of Pascal came with the rise of microcomputers like the Apple II and Tandy (3:21-3:32), especially with affordable integrated systems like Borland Turbo Pascal for around $50 (3:39-3:57).
Pascal vs. Basic: Wirth viewed Pascal as superior to Basic for pedagogical reasons and system development on early microprocessors (4:42-5:01).
Open Source Philosophy: Universities, including Wirth's, prioritized "spreading their ideas" (6:32-6:35) by distributing software freely, reflecting an early form of open-source behavior.
Modula-2 Development: Nine years after Pascal, Modula-2 was designed for system development, influenced by Xerox PARC's Mesa (7:07-7:20), introducing the crucial feature of modules with safe, separate compilation (7:27-8:04).
Oberon's Simplicity and Object Orientation: Oberon resulted from simplifying Modula-2, focusing on "simpler languages" for teaching (8:30-8:37), adding "type extension" (8:56-9:01) and leveraging existing "procedure variables" (9:08-9:12) to implement "full scheme of object orientation" (9:12-9:17).
Hardware Diversification: Inspired by his experience with a personal Alto computer at Xerox PARC (9:41-9:57), Wirth decided to build his own hardware (10:46-10:52), leading to the Lilith (11:42-11:46) and Ceres computers (11:59-12:00).
Modern Teaching with FPGAs: Wirth now applies "simplicity and well-structuredness" (12:13-12:17) principles to hardware using FPGAs (12:25-12:29), having implemented a simplified RISC processor (12:37-12:44) and an Oberon compiler for it (12:59-13:05), which he uses for teaching (13:13-13:20).
