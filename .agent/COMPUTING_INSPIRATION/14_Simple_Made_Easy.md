# 14. Simple Made Easy - Rich Hickey

**Speaker**: Rich Hickey  
**URL**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SxdOUGdseq4
**Duration**: 1 hour, 1 minute, 39 seconds  
**Channel**: Strange Loop Conference

---

Rich Hickey's "Simple Made Easy" keynote (0:04) highlights the critical distinction between "simple" and "easy" in software design. He argues that simplicity is objective (5:06), referring to something being "one fold" or "unbraided" (1:59) and free from intertwining (4:36). In contrast, "easy" is subjective (8:22), relating to familiarity or proximity (5:18), which can lead to complex and hard-to-maintain software in the long run. Hickey emphasizes that focusing on "easy" (18:39) in programming tools and practices, often driven by quick adoption or perceived familiarity, introduces incidental complexity (20:41) that ultimately slows down development (18:11) and makes systems unreliable (12:35). He advocates for choosing simple constructs that yield simpler artifacts (32:50) to build robust and maintainable software (33:27).

Here are the key takeaways from the talk:

Defining "Simple" and "Easy": Hickey defines "simple" as "one fold" or "unbraided," meaning not intertwined (1:59). "Easy" is defined as "to lie near" or "familiar" (2:40).
Objective vs. Relative: Simplicity is presented as an objective quality (5:06), while ease is relative and subjective (8:22), depending on an individual's familiarity or skill set.
Focus on Artifacts: The talk stresses that software should be assessed based on the complexity of its artifacts (9:36) (the running software) rather than the "easy" experience of creating the constructs (9:19) (the code itself).
Incidental Complexity: Focusing on "easy" tools can introduce "incidental complexity" (20:41)—complexity not inherent to the problem but introduced by the chosen tools or methods.
Long-term Impact: Prioritizing ease over simplicity leads to a slowdown in development over time (18:11) as complexity accumulates.
Avoiding "Complecting": Hickey introduces the term "complecting" (31:39) to describe the act of braiding or intertwining things together, which he asserts is detrimental to software quality (32:50).
Examples of Complex Constructs: He provides a list of common programming constructs that he considers complex, such as state and objects (29:27), methods (29:35), inheritance (30:02), and loops (30:33), contrasting them with simpler alternatives like values (29:30) and functions (29:37).
Path to Simplicity: Hickey argues that achieving simplicity requires a deliberate effort to avoid intertwining components and to choose constructs that inherently yield simpler artifacts (39:06).
